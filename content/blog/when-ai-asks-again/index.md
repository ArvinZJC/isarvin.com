In [‘At least it remembers’](https://aeon.co/essays/what-would-parenting-by-ai-look-like), an essay in *Aeon*, Tom Wooldridge writes about Maya, a teenager based on several young people he’s worked with. She tells her AI companion about her friend Jordan. A fortnight later, the AI asks how things are going with Jordan. Her mum works nights, and sometimes talking to the AI is the only way Maya can get to sleep.

I’ve wondered whether talking to AI more often might make me less inclined to message a friend. That assumes there’s someone I can message in the first place. What moved me about Maya’s story was that, on a quiet evening, she could pick up a conversation that mattered to her. The AI remembered who Jordan was.

I like how a chat with a friend can take an unexpected turn. They might text to ask how I am and end up sending me a voice note about something that’s been bothering them. Next time, I can be the one who asks how things are going. I imagine it would be lovely to hear ‘I missed you’ from someone I had feelings for. I’d know they’d been thinking of me too.

Should an AI companion be designed to keep me coming back, or to help me feel more confident on my own and closer to the people in my life?

Say I’m nervous about getting back in touch with an old friend. After five minutes of talking it through, I close the app and give my friend a ring. If that goes well, next time I might feel able to ring them straight away. I’d count that as a success, even if it meant I used the app less.

For Maya, a good evening might mean staying in the chat a little longer, until she feels settled enough to sleep. If those conversations stopped, she might miss them. The AI, for all it remembers about her, couldn’t miss her back. Can a relationship be psychologically valuable even when only one participant genuinely experiences it?
